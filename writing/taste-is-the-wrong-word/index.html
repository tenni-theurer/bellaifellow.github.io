“Taste” is the wrong word

By Sunil Paul · September 11, 2026 · First published on LinkedIn

Taste. It's the buzzword that's supposed to describe the last stand of human capability as thinking machines keep advancing. But it's the wrong word. It suggests something innate, which means you can't learn it or develop it. And it sounds like aesthetics and fashion.

For all their talents, these new thinking machines still need guidance on what to do, what to leave behind, and whether a thing should be done at all. What they need from us is discernment, judgment, and wisdom: deciding what is good, whether it was done right, and whether it should be done in the first place. The good news is that all three grow over time. They can be cultivated.

This has gotten personal for me. My kids are young adults entering the workforce, and they and their friends tell me it's very hard to find the entry-level jobs that were plentiful even five or ten years ago. I'm hearing the same from friends at law firms, technology companies, and consulting firms: they need fewer entry-level roles. Those jobs and the mentorship that came with them were where people used to build judgment.

Experienced people have a version of the same problem. I've spent a career building judgment about how products get built and how organizations grow and behave. A lot of that still holds. But AI changes enough of it that developing real judgment here means almost starting over, and doing it hands-on.

So how does judgment get built? By doing things, seeing what works and what fails, and working out why — in community, discussion, and argument with other people. By learning the underlying human values and arguments we've been having for hundreds of years. And by understanding how people in the past grappled with challenges like this one.

Learning the skills of AI is easy these days. There are plenty of videos and courses that will teach you to prompt well, run agents, build loops, and use the tools. There are no videos for building judgment, discernment, and wisdom. You have to get hands-on, with other people, to cultivate those.

That's the question Tenni Theurer and I have been working on, and it led to this fall's program of the Bell AI Fellowship: building judgment for the AI age. It's organized into three paths. Each is a different method for the same capacity.

Practice. You build things with AI, and you watch other people build them. The Homebrew AI Club is a working session where members demo their real setups, live — the agent, the pipeline, the experiment they ran — then say what worked and what broke since last time. Michael W. Albers, who ran product at Yahoo and AOL, came into an earlier cohort already building with AI. His words afterward: “I came out running on a system I built myself, and I use it every day now, with clients, in engagements, and in whatever comes next. A complete pivot in how I work.”

Principles. We read the people who asked today's AI questions first: Descartes, Turing, Hobbes, Wiener, Lovelace, Schelling. Short, dense texts, read twice, then two hours working out what they say. A moderator asks questions and stays out of the way. You leave with your own view, argued out against other people's.

Precedent. Nothing about a technology's speed is inevitable. Across twelve cases from the last century — nuclear power against commercial aviation, the Asilomar pause against the Crypto Wars, ridesharing against self-driving cars — we look at what sped things up, what redirected them, and what stopped them. Then we ask what that record says about where AI's path will be decided, and by whom.

Practice, principles, precedent. Three paths to the skills that matter most now. Not taste. But judgment, discernment, and wisdom: what is good, whether it was done right, and whether it should be done at all.
