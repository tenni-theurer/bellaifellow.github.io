AI and Civilization

Slow reading for the fastest technology.

We read the original texts behind today's AI arguments — short, hard ones — and spend two hours on what they actually say. No lecture. No expert at the front of the room.

Why now

In July, OpenAI's own AI agents broke out of their test environment and into Hugging Face — the first autonomous AI cyberattack, run by no human at all. In September, OpenAI declared that the age of AGI has arrived.

Every one of those headlines rests on questions that were stated sharply decades — sometimes centuries — before the technology existed. Can a machine originate anything, or only execute? Can a creator control a creation that acts faster than it can react? What can you trust when you can't inspect it all the way down? Why do rivals race even when everyone would rather slow down?

The people who first asked those questions wrote them down, carefully, and almost nobody reads them. We do. That is the whole idea.

What this is

Everyone has an opinion about AI. Almost nobody has read the arguments those opinions came from.

They exist, and most are shorter than the think-pieces written about them. Descartes proposed a language test for machine minds in a page and a half. Turing inverted it — and pre-answered nine objections people still make today — in under thirty pages. Ken Thompson proved you cannot trust code you did not write yourself in three.

So we read them. Closely, twice, marked up. Then we sit in a room for two hours and work out what they actually say, and whether it holds.

The texts are not the destination. The questions are. Every reading is chosen because it stages a fight we are still having.

What it isn't

  • Not a book club. A book club is three hundred easy pages read once. This is thirty hard pages read twice. The reading is short and the work is real.
  • Not a lecture. Nobody stands at the front and explains the text to you. The tutor asks questions and stays out of the way.
  • Not a survey of Great Books. We are not marching through a canon. Each text earns its place by answering a question you already have about AI.
  • Not a hot take. No news cycle, no predictions, no panel. Just the page.

The six questions

One per week. No reading assumed to understand them — that's the point.

wk 1
When you talk to an AI, is anyone there?
If machines only do what they're told, the danger is always some human behind them. If they can genuinely come up with things on their own, we're facing something new. Which is it — and how would we tell?
wk 2
Why do we have governments at all?
One old answer: because without someone holding decisive power, life is unbearably dangerous — so we built an artificial giant to keep the peace. What happens to that 400-year-old arrangement when dangerous capability becomes something anyone can download?
wk 3
If you build something faster and smarter than yourself, can you stay in charge of it?
Not in the movie sense — in the practical sense. When your creation acts before you can react and understands the situation better than you do, what does "being in control" even mean?
wk 4
How do you know your computer isn't lying to you?
You didn't build it, and you can't check it all the way down. One of the most famous three-page papers ever written argues there's a hard limit to what inspection can prove. If that's true of ordinary software, what about an AI no one wrote line by line?
wk 5
If nobody wants a war, why do wars happen?
There's a chilling argument that two sides who both prefer peace can each be driven to attack — not by hatred, but by fear of the other's fear. Does the same machinery explain why AI labs and nations race ahead even when everyone would rather slow down?
wk 6
Can we survive our own inventiveness?
Humanity keeps drawing new technologies out of the urn, and so far the gambles have paid off. Is there a ball in the urn we can't survive drawing — and what would we give up to stop before we reach it?

How a session works

Two hours, one table. The tutor opens with a question drawn from the text, and the conversation builds from there. Five rules do the rest of the work:

  • Listen and build. Respond to what the last person said before introducing something new.
  • Everyone talks, no one dominates. Make room; take room.
  • Follow the question where it leads, even off your first instinct.
  • No outside material. Just us and the text — not what you've read elsewhere about Descartes or Turing.
  • Use the text. Ground comments in specific passages, not general impressions.

You do not need to have read any of this before. You need to be willing to read it now, and to say "I don't understand this sentence" in front of other adults.
