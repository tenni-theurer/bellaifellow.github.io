How Society Shapes Technology

Nothing about a technology's speed is inevitable.

Thirteen episodes from 120 years of technology history — how public perception, regulation, and markets sped technologies up, slowed them down, redirected them, or stopped them cold. And what that record actually says about what happens to AI next.

Why now

In September, a bill to ban artificial superintelligence was introduced in the U.S. Congress. Data-center projects worth $130 billion a quarter are being blocked or delayed by local opposition. Every AI policy argument you hear — pause it, preempt the states, let the market decide — has been run before, on other technologies, with recorded outcomes.

Almost nobody in the AI debate knows those outcomes. Nuclear power was stopped for forty years by an accident that killed no one. Aviation regulation didn't slow the airplane down — it created the airline industry. The Asilomar pause on gene splicing held; the encryption export ban collapsed. The pattern of which interventions work, and which backfire, is knowable. We're going to know it.

The thirteen cases

Every case gets the same five-part treatment: the technology's trajectory, how the public saw it, what regulators did, what markets did, and the verdict — sped up, redirected, or stopped.

sped
Ridesharing · Social media · Aviation safety · The Space Race
Where intervention (or its engineered absence) added speed — including regulation that built the industry it governed
slowed
Autonomous vehicles · Crypto · Encryption · Nuclear weapons
Redirected or capped — nine nuclear states instead of the predicted twenty-five
stopped
U.S. nuclear power · Chemical weapons · European GMOs · Supersonic flight · CFCs
Halted for decades — by verified treaty, consumer veto, a 52-year rule, or substitution

A few of the lessons

  • Regulation is more often a redirect than a brake — and sometimes an accelerant. The interesting question is never "regulate or not," it's which design ages well.
  • A vivid accident reprices an industry only when it confirms a pre-existing suspicion. Three Mile Island killed no one and froze nuclear power for forty years; the first self-driving fatality ended one company, not the field.
  • Cover-ups convert accidents into catastrophes. The second-order event always outdamages the first-order harm.
  • Pauses hold under knowable conditions — few actors, a bright line, verifiable compliance, practitioner consensus, and a chokepoint. Asilomar held. Encryption controls failed. Where does an AI pause land?
  • One book, one whistleblower, or one measurement can outweigh a decade of PR. Silent Spring, strontium-90 in baby teeth, the Facebook Files. AI's version hasn't appeared yet — when it does, it sets the terms for years.
